The FHSA: how Canada's first home savings account works

The First Home Savings Account combines a tax deduction going in with tax-free withdrawals coming out. Here is how it works and how it fits with the Home Buyers' Plan.

The First Home Savings Account (FHSA) was created to help Canadians save for a first home. It combines features of the RRSP and the TFSA: contributions are tax-deductible, and withdrawals used to buy a qualifying first home are tax-free. For many first-time buyers it is the most powerful savings account available.

The key numbers

FeatureDetail
Annual contribution limit$8,000
Lifetime limit$40,000
Carry-forwardUp to $8,000 of unused room, so up to $16,000 in one year
ContributionsTax-deductible, like an RRSP
Qualifying withdrawalsTax-free, like a TFSA
Time limitUp to 15 years, or until the end of the year you turn 71

Room starts building only once you open the account. There is no catch-up for the years before you opened it, so opening an account early, even with a small deposit, starts your room.

Who can open one?

To open an FHSA you generally need to be:

  • A Canadian resident
  • At least 18 years old (and under 71)
  • A first-time home buyer, meaning that you did not live in a home you owned, or that your spouse or common-law partner owned, at any time in the current year or the previous four calendar years

Each person has their own account, so a couple can each save up to the limit and claim two deductions.

How the tax benefits work

Put $8,000 into your FHSA, and you can deduct it from your income for that year. If your marginal tax rate is 30%, that deduction could mean about $2,400 back. The investments inside grow without being taxed, and if you use the money toward a qualifying first home, you take it out tax-free. You can also choose to keep a deduction for a later year, which can be useful if your income is expected to rise.

Using the FHSA with the Home Buyers’ Plan

The FHSA and the RRSP Home Buyers’ Plan (HBP) are separate programs, and you can use both for the same purchase. The HBP lets you borrow up to $60,000 from your RRSP to buy or build a home, which you repay over time, while the FHSA withdrawal is tax-free and has no repayment. Together with your TFSA, these accounts can add up to a larger down payment than any one of them could. Rules for each program have conditions, so confirm them before you withdraw.

What if you do not buy a home?

You are not locked in. If you decide not to buy, you can transfer the balance to your RRSP or RRIF without using up your RRSP contribution room, and the money keeps growing tax-sheltered. You can also withdraw it as taxable income. That makes opening an FHSA a low-risk step, even if your plans are not final.

Things to watch for

  • Over-contributing is taxed at 1% a month on the excess, so check your room.
  • Timing the withdrawal. Qualifying withdrawals need a written agreement to buy or build a home, and you generally must move in within a set time after buying.
  • Investment choices. An FHSA is an account, not an investment. If you plan to buy within a few years, keep the investments conservative so a market dip does not shrink your down payment.
  • Your bank’s fees and rules. Compare providers before opening one.

Putting it together

  1. Open an FHSA as soon as you qualify to start your room.
  2. Contribute as much as you can toward the annual limit, and claim the deduction.
  3. Use your tax refund to build your TFSA or pay down debt.
  4. Check what you can borrow, and how much you need, with a mortgage pre-approval before you shop.
  5. Budget for closing costs as well as the down payment. We cover those in our article on buying a home in Ontario.

If you are planning a purchase, we can look at your savings, income and mortgage options together, and help you decide how to use each account.

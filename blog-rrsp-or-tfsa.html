RRSP or TFSA: where should your next dollar go?

Both are registered accounts, but they save tax at different times. A plain comparison, with the 2026 limits, to help you choose.

InvestingOctober 1, 20266 min readBy Shreem Financial Group

In this article

  1. How the RRSP works
  2. How the TFSA works
  3. Side by side
  4. How to choose
  5. Things to check first

The RRSP and the TFSA are the two most common ways Canadians save and invest with a tax advantage. They look similar, but they work in opposite ways. Choosing between them, or splitting your money across both, comes down to your income today, your income later, and what the money is for.

How the RRSP works

When you contribute to a Registered Retirement Savings Plan, you get a tax deduction. That lowers your taxable income for the year, often producing a refund. Your investments grow without being taxed each year, and you pay income tax on whatever you withdraw. The idea is to take the deduction when your income, and tax rate, is high, and pay the tax later when your income is lower, usually in retirement.

  • Contribution room: 18% of the previous year’s earned income, up to a yearly maximum ($33,810 for 2026), less any pension adjustment. Unused room carries forward.
  • Deadline: contributions made in the first 60 days of a year can be claimed on the previous year’s return.
  • Age limit: the plan must be converted to a RRIF or annuity by the end of the year you turn 71.
  • Special uses: the Home Buyers’ Plan lets first-time buyers borrow from their RRSP, and the Lifelong Learning Plan helps fund education, both repaid over time.

How the TFSA works

A Tax-Free Savings Account gives no deduction when you put money in. In return, growth and withdrawals are generally tax-free. You can take money out whenever you like, and the amount you withdraw is added back to your room in the next calendar year.

  • 2026 limit: $7,000. If you have been eligible since the program began in 2009 and never contributed, total room is $109,000.
  • Eligibility: Canadian residents who are 18 or older. Room builds from the year you turn 18.
  • Flexibility: withdrawals do not count as income, so they do not affect income-tested benefits such as Old Age Security or the Guaranteed Income Supplement.
  • Watch out: going over your room triggers a tax of 1% a month on the excess, so check your room on your CRA My Account.

How to choose

The RRSP tends to win if your income is high now and you expect it to be lower in retirement, because the deduction saves you tax at a high rate and you pay it back at a lower one. It is also the natural home for employer matching programs and for saving for retirement specifically.

The TFSA tends to win if your income is modest today, if you may need the money before retirement, or if you want withdrawals that will not raise your taxable income later. It is also the easier account to use for goals such as an emergency fund, a car or a home.

Many people use both. A good routine is to contribute to the RRSP and put the resulting refund into the TFSA, so the tax saving is not spent.

Things to check first

  • Your room for each account. Both numbers are in your CRA My Account.
  • Employer matching. A group RRSP match is free money, so capture it before anything else.
  • High-interest debt. Paying off a credit card at 20% often beats any investment return.
  • What the money is for. Retirement, a home purchase and a short-term goal point to different accounts, including the First Home Savings Account for a first home.
  • What you invest in. Both are accounts, not investments. A TFSA full of cash earns very little, and the right mix of investments depends on your timeline and comfort with risk.

The right mix changes as your income and life change. We can look at your room, your income and your goals together and suggest an order for your savings.

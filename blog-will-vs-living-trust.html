Will or living trust: what is the difference?

A will is a good start, but it is not the whole estate plan for every family. Here is what each one does, and what neither can do alone.

Many Canadians assume a will covers everything. It covers a lot, but it works only after you pass away, and in most cases it goes through probate, the court process that confirms the will and gives your executor the authority to act. A living trust works differently. Knowing where each one fits helps you decide what your family actually needs.

What a will does

A will is a written set of instructions that takes effect at death. In Ontario, a properly signed and witnessed will can:

  • Name an executor (the person who carries out your wishes, pays debts and distributes your assets)
  • Say who receives your property, from the house to personal items
  • Name guardians for minor children
  • Set up a testamentary trust, so money for young children or a family member with special needs is managed over time rather than handed over at once

Everyone with a spouse, children or property should have one. Without a will, Ontario’s intestacy rules decide who inherits, and they may not match what you would have chosen. A court may also have to appoint someone to look after your estate.

What probate means in Ontario

For most estates the executor applies to the court for a certificate of appointment of estate trustee, often called probate. Banks and land registry offices commonly ask to see it before they release or transfer assets. Ontario charges an estate administration tax on the value of the estate that passes through the will. Estates of $50,000 or less are exempt, and above that the tax is roughly 1.5% of the value. On a larger estate that is a real cost, and the process can take weeks or months.

Probate is not a disaster, and plenty of estates go through it without trouble. But it is public, it takes time, and some families would rather avoid it where it is sensible to do so.

What a living trust does

A living trust (also called an inter vivos trust) is set up while you are alive. You, as the settlor, transfer certain assets into the trust, and a trustee manages them for the beneficiaries under rules you write down. Because those assets are legally held by the trust, they may avoid probate, can be passed on faster and stay more private than assets that go through a will.

A trust can also help when:

  • Beneficiaries are young or need support over time
  • You own property in more than one province or country
  • You want to plan for incapacity, since a trustee can keep managing the assets if you cannot
  • Your family situation is blended or complicated

The trade-offs of a trust

A living trust is not a free upgrade. It usually costs more to set up, it needs a lawyer who knows trust law, and it has to be run properly: the trust files its own tax return each year, and moving a home or investments into it can have tax consequences. Transferring certain assets, such as a property with a mortgage, may need the lender’s agreement. For many families a will plus good beneficiary designations is enough. A trust tends to earn its cost where the estate is larger, the family is more complex, or privacy and planning for incapacity matter.

Assets that pass outside your will

Some of your assets may never go through the will at all, which is why the whole picture matters:

  • Accounts and policies with a named beneficiary: life insurance, RRSPs, RRIFs, TFSAs and many pensions go directly to the person named, usually without probate.
  • Jointly held property with right of survivorship: a home or bank account owned jointly generally passes to the surviving owner.
  • Assets already in a trust.

This is useful, but it also causes problems. An old beneficiary designation, such as an ex-spouse on a policy, overrides what the will says. It is worth checking each one.

Where life insurance fits

Life insurance proceeds paid to a named beneficiary are generally tax-free and arrive without waiting for probate. That makes insurance a practical way to give your family cash quickly, to pay final taxes and debts, to equalize an inheritance when one child receives a home or a business, or to fund a trust. If your estate holds illiquid assets such as a house, a business or a cottage, planning for cash is often the missing piece.

Where to start

  1. List what you own and how each asset is held (sole name, joint, in a corporation).
  2. Check who is named as beneficiary on every account and policy.
  3. Make sure you have a current will and, in Ontario, remember that marriage cancels an earlier will unless it was made in contemplation of that marriage.
  4. Put powers of attorney in place for property and for personal care, so someone can act if you are unable to.
  5. Talk to an estate lawyer about the right documents for your situation.

Will or living trust: what is the difference?

A will is a good start, but it is not the whole estate plan for every family.

Many Canadians assume a will covers everything. It covers a lot, but it works only after you pass away, and it usually goes through probate, which is the court process that confirms the will and gives your executor authority to act. In Ontario, probate comes with fees and can take months.

What a will does. It names an executor, says who receives your assets, names guardians for minor children, and can record your wishes. Everyone with a family should have one.

What a living trust does. A living trust (also called an inter vivos trust) is set up while you are alive. You transfer certain assets into it, and it manages and passes them on under rules you set. Because those assets are held by the trust, they may avoid probate, transfer faster and stay more private than assets that pass through a will. A trust can also help when beneficiaries are young or need support over time.

The trade-offs. Trusts usually cost more to set up, need more care to run, and do not suit every situation. Some assets already pass outside your will, such as accounts and policies with a named beneficiary, or property held jointly with a right of survivorship. A good plan looks at all of these together.

Where to start. List what you own, check who is named as beneficiary on each account and policy, and talk to an estate lawyer about the right documents. We can help with the financial side: how your investments, insurance and property fit the plan, and whether life insurance should be there to cover taxes, debts or equal shares for your family.

What does a FIRE journey really mean?

FIRE stands for financial independence, retire early. Here is how to think about it without the hype.

FIRE is about reaching the point where your savings and investments can cover your living costs, so work becomes a choice. For some people that means retiring at 45. For many others it means more freedom to change careers, take time off or work part time earlier than they could have.

The simple idea. A common rule of thumb is that you need about 25 times your yearly spending invested, which comes from withdrawing around 4% a year. It is a starting point, not a promise. Markets, inflation, taxes and how long you need the money to last all change the answer.

Your savings rate matters most. The more of your income you save, the less you need to live on and the sooner you get there. Small changes early, like paying down expensive debt or saving a raise instead of spending it, make a bigger difference than chasing returns.

Use the right accounts. In Canada, the TFSA, RRSP and RESP each save tax in a different way, and a non-registered account fills in the rest. Which one comes first depends on your income and goals.

Plan for the risks. Retiring early means more years without a paycheque. That makes disability and critical illness cover, health benefits, and a plan for your mortgage as important as the investments.

We start every plan with where you are today, then work out the steps toward your own version of financial independence.

Five questions to ask before you renew your mortgage

Your lender's renewal letter is an offer, not the only option.

Most renewal letters arrive well before your term ends, and many people sign without comparing. A few questions can save real money.

1. What rate and term are you being offered? Ask for it in writing and compare it with what other lenders offer for the same term.

2. Can you switch lenders at renewal? At the end of your term you can usually move to another lender without a prepayment penalty, though there can be legal or admin costs. Check your own mortgage terms.

3. How do prepayment options work? Look at how much extra you can pay each year, and whether you can increase your payments, without a penalty.

4. What happens if you sell or refinance during the term? Penalties differ between lenders and between fixed and variable mortgages. Knowing the formula before you sign avoids surprises.

5. Does the new payment fit your budget? Rates may have changed since your last term. Make sure the payment works alongside your other goals, such as RESP, RRSP and insurance.

Comparing at least two other options often shows whether your renewal offer is competitive. We can do that comparison with you before your term ends.

RRSP or TFSA: where should your next dollar go?

Both are registered accounts, but they save tax at different times.

The RRSP gives you a tax deduction when you contribute. Your money grows tax-sheltered, and you pay tax when you withdraw it, ideally when your income is lower, such as in retirement.

The TFSA gives no deduction, but growth and withdrawals are generally tax-free, and you can take money out when you need it. That makes it flexible for goals both near and far.

How to choose. If your income is high now and you expect it to be lower later, the RRSP deduction is often worth more. If your income is modest or you want flexibility, the TFSA can fit better. Many people use both, and a refund from an RRSP contribution can go into a TFSA.

Things to check first. Your contribution room for each account, any employer plan with matching, high-interest debt you could pay off first, and whether the money is for retirement, a home or something sooner.

Ask your advisor to look at your room and goals before you choose. The right mix changes as your income and life change.

Term or permanent life insurance?

Two types of life insurance do different jobs. Here is how to tell which one fits.

Term insurance covers you for a set number of years, often the length of a mortgage or the years your children depend on you. It usually costs less for the same amount of cover, which makes it a popular first choice for young families.

Permanent insurance is designed to last your whole life, as long as premiums are paid. It costs more, and it can support estate and legacy planning, for example by providing money to cover taxes at death or to leave a gift to your family.

How people combine them. Many families use term to protect big debts and income now, and add permanent cover later when their needs and budget are clearer.

Questions to ask. How much would your family need if your income stopped? How long will they depend on it? Can the policy be converted or renewed? What happens to the price over time?

An advisor can compare both types from several insurers, so you pick cover that fits your family and your budget.

What does a FIRE journey really mean?

FIRE stands for financial independence, retire early. Here is how the idea works in Canada, what the numbers look like, and the questions to ask before you start.

RetirementOctober 6, 20267 min readBy Shreem Financial Group

In this article

  2. Different kinds of FIRE
  3. What is different in Canada
  4. The risks people forget

FIRE stands for financial independence, retire early. The idea is simple: save and invest enough that your investments can cover your living costs, so work becomes a choice rather than a requirement. It does not have to mean quitting at 40. For many people it means having options: switching careers, working part-time, taking a break or retiring ten years sooner than they otherwise could.

The basic math

Most FIRE plans start with two numbers: how much you spend in a year, and how much you need invested to cover it.

A common rule of thumb is the 4% rule. It comes from US research on how much a portfolio could pay out each year over a 30-year retirement without running out. Flip it around and you get a target of about 25 times your annual spending. If you spend $60,000 a year, that points to roughly $1.5 million.

It is a starting point, not a promise. People who retire at 40 may need to cover 50 or more years, markets do not behave like a spreadsheet, and Canadian costs and taxes differ from the American data the rule came from. Many people use a lower withdrawal rate to build in a cushion.

Different kinds of FIRE

StyleWhat it means
Lean FIREA very frugal lifestyle with a smaller target
Fat FIREA comfortable lifestyle with room to spend, so a larger target
Barista FIREPart-time or lower-stress work covers some costs while investments cover the rest
Coast FIREYou have saved enough early that growth alone should reach your retirement goal, so you only need to earn your current living costs

What is different in Canada

Canadians have tools and rules that change the plan:

  • Registered accounts. The TFSA, RRSP and the First Home Savings Account shelter growth from tax, and the order you draw from them matters. Withdrawals from an RRSP or RRIF are taxed as income, while TFSA withdrawals are not.
  • CPP and OAS. Canada Pension Plan benefits can start as early as 60, but taking them before 65 reduces them by 0.6% for each month early, up to a 36% reduction. Waiting past 65 raises them by 0.7% a month, up to 42% at 70. Old Age Security starts at 65 and can be deferred to 70 for a higher monthly amount. If you retire at 45, those benefits are a long way off, so your plan has to bridge the gap.
  • Health coverage. Provincial health plans cover doctors and hospitals but not most prescription drugs, dental care, vision or travel outside Canada. Leaving a job often means losing a group benefits plan, so budget for individual coverage.

The risks people forget

  • Sequence of returns risk. A market drop in the first few years of withdrawals hurts far more than the same drop later.
  • Inflation. Spending of $60,000 today will cost much more in 25 years.
  • Healthcare and long-term care costs that rise with age.
  • Taxes on withdrawals, which depend on which accounts you draw from and when.
  • Life changes: children, divorce, illness or a parent who needs care.

Protecting the plan with insurance

Early retirement plans often leave out the things that can derail them. If you are still building your savings, disability insurance protects your ability to keep contributing if you cannot work. Critical illness cover can pay a lump sum to avoid raiding your investments. Life insurance protects a partner or children who depend on your plan. These cost money, but they are part of what makes a plan sturdy rather than lucky.

A simple way to begin

  1. Track your spending for two or three months so your number is real, not guessed.
  2. Pick a target using your annual spending and a conservative withdrawal rate.
  3. Set your savings rate. This is the biggest lever you control. Raising it shortens the timeline from both ends: you save more, and you need less.
  4. Use your accounts in a sensible order, such as employer matching first, then TFSA, RRSP and FHSA depending on your goals and income.
  5. Review once a year. A plan on paper is only useful if you update it.

FIRE is not for everyone, and it is not all or nothing. Even moving your retirement date a few years earlier can change how you live now. If you would like help turning the idea into numbers, we can map your income, savings and goals and show what different timelines would look like.

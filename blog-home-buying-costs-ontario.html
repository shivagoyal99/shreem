Buying a home in Ontario: the costs beyond the down payment

Land transfer tax, legal fees, insurance and more. A realistic look at what you will pay at closing, with a worked example.

Real estateSeptember 22, 20267 min readBy Shreem Financial Group

In this article

  2. Ontario land transfer tax
  3. A worked example
  4. Other closing costs to budget for
  5. Remember the ongoing costs

When you plan to buy a home, the down payment gets most of the attention. But the cash you need at closing is often higher than people expect. Knowing the extra costs ahead of time helps you set a realistic budget and avoid last-minute surprises.

The down payment

For a mortgage backed by default insurance, the minimum down payment in Canada is 5% of the first $500,000 of the price and 10% of the part above $500,000. Homes priced at $1.5 million or more need at least 20% down. If you put down less than 20%, your mortgage must be insured, and the premium is a percentage of the loan:

Down paymentInsurance premium (percent of the loan)
5% to 9.99%4.00%
10% to 14.99%3.10%
15% to 19.99%2.80%
20% or moreNone required

The premium is usually added to your mortgage, and Ontario charges provincial sales tax on it, which must be paid in cash at closing. Rules and limits are updated from time to time, so confirm the current ones before you buy.

Ontario land transfer tax

The largest closing cost for most buyers is the provincial land transfer tax. It is calculated in brackets on the purchase price:

Portion of the priceRate
First $55,0000.5%
$55,000 to $250,0001.0%
$250,000 to $400,0001.5%
$400,000 to $2,000,0002.0%
Over $2,000,0002.5%

First-time buyers can claim a refund of up to $4,000, which fully covers the tax on a home up to about $368,000. If you buy in the City of Toronto, you also pay a separate municipal land transfer tax of similar size. Mississauga and most other GTA cities do not charge one.

Other closing costs to budget for

  • Legal fees and disbursements. Your lawyer or notary handles the title search, the transfer and the mortgage registration. Plan on roughly $1,500 to $3,000 depending on the deal.
  • Title insurance. A one-time premium, often a few hundred dollars, that protects against title problems.
  • Home inspection. Typically several hundred dollars, and well worth it before you remove conditions.
  • Appraisal. Some lenders charge for one, and some include it.
  • Adjustments. You reimburse the seller for prepaid property taxes or utilities, and the sellers do the same for you.
  • Home insurance. Your lender will require proof of coverage before closing.
  • Moving costs and immediate repairs, such as new locks, painting or appliances.

As a rough guide, closing costs often add up to between 1.5% and 4% of the purchase price, before moving costs. The number depends on the price, where the home is, and whether you are a first-time buyer.

Remember the ongoing costs

Closing costs are one-time, but owning a home brings regular costs as well: property tax, utilities, insurance, condo fees if you buy a condo, and maintenance. A common guideline is to set aside 1% of the home’s value each year for repairs. Lenders also look at these costs when they decide how much you can borrow.

How to prepare

  1. Get a mortgage pre-approval so you know your price range and your rate is held while you shop.
  2. Use an FHSA and a TFSA to build your down payment and closing costs, and check whether the Home Buyers’ Plan fits.
  3. Keep a separate cushion for closing costs, apart from your down payment.
  4. Protect the purchase. Life and disability insurance can keep the mortgage payments going if something happens to your income.

Five questions to ask before you renew your mortgage

Your lender's renewal letter is an offer, not the only option. These five questions help you compare it properly before you sign.

MortgagesOctober 3, 20266 min readBy Shreem Financial Group

In this article

  1. What rate and term am I really being offered?
  2. Should I stay, switch or refinance?
  3. What would it cost to leave early?
  4. How much flexibility do I have?
  5. Does the mortgage still fit my life?
  6. A simple timeline

A few months before your term ends, your lender will send a renewal offer. It is easy to sign and send it back. But the rate in that letter is often not the best one you could get, and renewal is one of the few times you can change lenders, adjust your term or restructure your mortgage without much friction. These five questions help you use it well.

1. What rate and term am I really being offered?

Look past the headline rate. Check the term (one, three, five years or longer), whether the rate is fixed or variable, and how the offer compares with what new customers are getting. Lenders often keep a higher “renewal rate” for existing customers who do not ask. A mortgage broker can compare rates across many lenders, and you can use a better offer to negotiate with your current one.

Federally regulated lenders must send your renewal statement within a window before your term ends, typically several weeks to a few months ahead. Do not wait until the last week to start looking.

2. Should I stay, switch or refinance?

You generally have three choices:

OptionWhat happensGood fit when
Renew with your lenderSame lender, new rate and termTheir offer is competitive and you want it done quickly
Switch at renewalMove to a new lender when the term endsAnother lender offers a clearly better rate or features
RefinanceChange the mortgage itself: borrow more, change the amortization, consolidate debtYou need cash, want to restructure, or your needs have changed

One point worth knowing: at renewal, a straight switch to another federally regulated lender, with no increase in the amount owing and no longer amortization, generally does not require you to pass the mortgage stress test again. That makes switching more practical than people expect. Refinancing, on the other hand, normally means qualifying again.

3. What would it cost to leave early?

If there is any chance you will sell, refinance or move before the term ends, find out the prepayment penalty before you sign. As a general guide, a variable-rate mortgage usually charges three months of interest. A fixed-rate mortgage charges the greater of three months of interest or the interest rate differential (IRD), and the IRD can be much larger, especially with a lender that calculates it on posted rates. Ask how your lender calculates it, in writing.

4. How much flexibility do I have?

Two mortgages with the same rate can behave very differently. Ask about:

  • Prepayment privileges: how much extra you can pay each year without a penalty, as a lump sum or by raising your payments
  • Payment frequency: accelerated biweekly payments make the equivalent of one extra monthly payment a year
  • Portability: whether you can move the mortgage to a new home without a penalty
  • Open or closed: an open mortgage can be repaid at any time but usually carries a higher rate
  • Home equity line of credit: whether it is registered as part of the mortgage

5. Does the mortgage still fit my life?

Five years is a long time. Since you signed, your income may have changed, you may have had children, started a business or want to buy a second property. Think about where you will be at the end of the next term:

  • Will you move, upsize or downsize?
  • Do you want to pay it off faster, or free up monthly cash flow instead?
  • Are you carrying higher-interest debt that could be consolidated?
  • Do you have life, disability or critical illness insurance in place so the mortgage is protected if something happens to you?

That last point matters more than many people realize. A mortgage is often the biggest debt a family has, and protecting the ability to keep paying it is part of a sound plan. Insurance through your lender is usually tied to the loan and can be reduced or cancelled with it, whereas an individual policy stays with you.

A simple timeline

  1. 120 to 150 days before maturity: start looking at rates and options.
  2. When the offer arrives: compare it with other lenders, and ask yours to match.
  3. Before you sign: check the term, penalty rules and privileges.
  4. After you renew: put the new maturity date in your calendar.
